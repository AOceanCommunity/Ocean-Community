What Sustainable Seafood Actually Means

Ocean Community·4 min read

A sustainable seafood label sounds like marketing until you look at what fisheries actually have to prove to earn one. The gap between certified and uncertified fisheries turns out to be significant.

What a certification like MSC actually checks

The Marine Stewardship Council certifies wild caught fisheries against a standard built around three things. Whether the targeted fish population stays healthy enough to keep replenishing itself, how much damage the fishing method causes to the surrounding ecosystem and how well the fishery is actually managed and enforced.

The gap between certified and uncertified

Research comparing certified and uncertified fisheries found that only about 9 percent of MSC certified fish stocks had fallen below a healthy biomass limit, compared to between 26 and 33 percent of stocks that were not MSC certified. Roughly a fifth of the world's wild marine catch is either certified to the standard or currently being assessed against it.

Only about 9 percent of MSC certified fish stocks are overfished, compared to 26 to 33 percent of stocks without that certification.

The bigger picture is more mixed

A 2019 United Nations report found that 82 percent of global fish landings by volume came from stocks considered sustainably managed, a share that had grown slightly in recent years. That number covers all managed fisheries, not just certified ones. It still leaves a meaningful share of the world's seafood coming from stocks that are not being managed sustainably at all.

What this actually means at the table

A sustainable seafood label is not meaningless the way some hotel eco badges are. It reflects an actual audit against a specific standard, repeated regularly rather than granted once. Looking for a recognized certification mark is a more reliable shortcut than trusting a menu description alone.
