What Creates an Ocean Dead Zone

Ocean Community·4 min read

Every summer, an area of the Gulf of Mexico roughly the size of a small country loses enough oxygen that crabs, shrimp and fish either leave or die where they sit. Nothing dumps poison there directly. The cause starts hundreds of miles upstream, on farmland that has nothing visibly to do with the ocean.

Fertilizer finishes its trip in the water

Nitrogen and phosphorus from fertilizer, applied across the Mississippi River watershed, washes off farmland during rain and drains into streams that feed the river. By the time that water reaches the Gulf, it carries a concentrated load of nutrients that a patch of open ocean would never naturally receive. Rivers draining other heavily farmed regions, including the Baltic Sea and China's coastal waters, produce the same effect at a similar scale.

From nutrients to no oxygen

Those nutrients feed explosive blooms of algae at the surface. When the algae die, they sink and decompose. That decomposition consumes dissolved oxygen from the surrounding water. In a well mixed part of the ocean, fresh oxygen would replace what gets used. In the Gulf, warm, less dense freshwater from the river sits in a layer on top of colder, saltier seawater. That layering prevents the two from mixing. Oxygen used up near the bottom simply never gets replenished, creating a zone of water too depleted for most species to survive.

The Gulf of Mexico's dead zone has measured as large as 8,776 square miles in a single summer. It forms when fertilizer runoff from Mississippi River farmland triggers algae blooms that strip oxygen from the water as they decompose.

What a fish can and cannot do about it

Mobile species such as shrimp and many fish swim out of a forming dead zone, which concentrates them at its edges in a pattern commercial fishing fleets have learned to track. Less mobile bottom dwellers, including worms, clams and some crab species, cannot relocate and die in place once oxygen drops low enough. A National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration survey team maps the Gulf zone's size every summer by dragging oxygen sensors along a fixed grid of stations, a measurement that has been repeated annually since the early 1980s.

A problem with a known fix that is hard to apply

The fix is not mysterious: reduce the fertilizer runoff reaching the river system, through buffer strips of vegetation along waterways, cover crops that hold nutrients in the soil and more precisely timed fertilizer application. A federal and state task force set a target in 2001 to shrink the Gulf dead zone to under 2,000 square miles. The zone has exceeded that target most years since, because the fix requires coordinated change across millions of acres of farmland spanning more than two dozen states, far beyond what any single regulation can mandate.
