Why the Ocean Glows Blue When You Disturb It at Night

Ocean Community·4 min read

A paddle dragged through calm water at night sometimes leaves a trail of pale blue light behind it, fading within a second or two. It looks ornamental, even inviting. What is actually happening is closer to an alarm going off: a single celled organism has just been physically jolted and is trying to make itself as visible as possible to whatever disturbed it.

A chemical reaction, not a glow stick

Bioluminescence is produced through a chemical reaction between a light emitting molecule called luciferin and an enzyme called luciferase, a pairing found across a wide range of unrelated marine organisms that evolved the ability independently rather than inheriting it from a shared ancestor. When luciferin reacts with oxygen in the presence of luciferase, it releases energy as light rather than heat, a strikingly efficient process compared to how most artificial light sources work. The specific chemistry varies between species, but the underlying principle stays the same across all of them.

Why a dinoflagellate wants to be seen

The blue glow behind a disturbed wake usually comes from dinoflagellates, single celled plankton present in surface water across much of the world's oceans. When physically jostled by a wave, a swimmer or a passing fish, a dinoflagellate flashes light as what researchers describe as a burglar alarm response, intended not to scare off the direct threat but to attract a larger predator that will go after whatever disturbed the dinoflagellate in the first place. The flash effectively outsources the dinoflagellate's defense to something higher up the food chain.

A dinoflagellate's flash of light when disturbed is thought to function as a burglar alarm, drawing in a larger predator to attack whatever threatened it rather than defending itself directly.

Different depths, entirely different purposes

Surface dinoflagellates use light mainly for startling a predator or signaling for help, but bioluminescence serves very different purposes at greater depth. In the deep ocean, where no sunlight reaches at all, an anglerfish dangles a glowing lure to draw prey within striking range, while some species of squid and small fish use light produced on their undersides, a strategy called counterillumination, to match the faint light filtering down from above and erase their silhouette from predators hunting from below. The same basic chemistry gets put to opposite uses depending on what an organism needs to survive at its particular depth.

A visible marker for an invisible bloom

Large concentrations of bioluminescent dinoflagellates occasionally produce visible glowing waves along certain coastlines, an event that draws tourists to specific bays known for the phenomenon at predictable times of year. The same organisms responsible for this spectacle can, under different nutrient and temperature conditions, form harmful algal blooms, since many bioluminescent dinoflagellate species belong to groups capable of both behaviors depending on local conditions. A glowing wave and a red tide can, in some cases, trace back to populations of the same genus.
