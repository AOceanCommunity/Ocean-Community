Why Kelp Forests Are Disappearing and What Replaces Them

Ocean Community·4 min read

Most of the attention on disappearing ocean habitat goes to coral reefs. Kelp forests get far less of it, despite covering roughly a quarter of the world's coastlines and supporting a similar density of fish, invertebrates and mammals. Several major kelp forests have collapsed in the last fifteen years. The cause is not always the water temperature alone.

A forest that grows two feet a day

Giant kelp is one of the fastest growing organisms on the planet, capable of adding two feet of length in a single day under good conditions and reaching heights of over 100 feet from the seafloor to the surface. A healthy kelp forest functions the way a terrestrial forest does: a dense canopy near the surface, an understory of smaller algae and a seafloor layer sheltering juvenile fish, abalone and sea stars. Sea otters, when present, are a key part of what keeps the system balanced.

The urchin barren

Sea urchins eat kelp. Under normal conditions, predators such as sea otters and sunflower sea stars keep urchin populations low enough that kelp can outgrow the grazing. Remove the predators and the balance flips. Off the coast of Northern California, a sea star wasting disease outbreak beginning in 2013 killed off sunflower sea stars across the region. A marine heatwave the following year weakened the kelp itself. Purple sea urchin numbers rose more than fortyfold in some areas. The result: over 95 percent of the region's bull kelp forest was gone within a few years, replaced by what scientists call an urchin barren, a seafloor covered in grazing urchins with almost nothing left for them to eat.

Tasmania's giant kelp, nearly gone

Tasmania's giant kelp forests tell a related but distinct story. Warming water flowing down from the Tasman Sea has pushed temperatures in the region well above what giant kelp tolerates. The forests that once lined the island's east coast have shrunk by more than 95 percent since the 1950s. Unlike the California case, the collapse here is driven primarily by direct heat stress rather than a grazer population explosion, which means the usual urchin culling and predator restoration efforts used elsewhere have limited effect. Giant kelp simply cannot grow in water that warm.

Why the loss matters beyond the kelp itself

A kelp forest's structure supports the same density of species that makes a coral reef valuable: nursery habitat for commercially fished species, carbon storage and a buffer that reduces wave energy reaching the coastline. When a forest collapses into an urchin barren, those functions mostly disappear along with it. Recovery is not guaranteed even once the original stressor eases. Urchin barrens can persist for decades once established, since a handful of surviving kelp spores struggle to establish in a seafloor already dense with grazers.
