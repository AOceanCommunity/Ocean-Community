Seagrass Meadows Are Vanishing at 1.5 Percent a Year

Ocean Community·4 min read

Seagrass meadows cover a sliver of the ocean floor yet do an outsized share of its carbon storage work. They are also disappearing fast enough that entire meadows are turning from carbon sinks into carbon sources.

An outsized carbon store

Seagrass meadows cover less than 0.2 percent of the total area of the ocean, yet the carbon buried in their soil and roots accounts for somewhere between 10 and 18 percent of all the carbon stored in ocean sediment worldwide. Measured per unit of area, a healthy seagrass meadow can store as much carbon as a tropical rainforest, sometimes more, most of it locked away in the soil beneath the grass rather than in the leaves themselves.

Nearly a third of it is already gone

Seagrass has lost an estimated 30 percent of its historical global coverage. The losses are continuing at around 1.5 percent of remaining coverage every year. Roughly 29 percent of the seagrass meadows scientists have assessed worldwide are already considered degraded. About 14 percent of seagrass species are now at risk of extinction.

Seagrass meadows cover less than 0.2 percent of the ocean floor but hold up to 18 percent of all the carbon buried in the sea, a ratio matched by almost no other ecosystem on Earth.

What happens when a meadow disappears

A living seagrass meadow keeps pulling carbon out of the water and locking it into the soil beneath it. A dead or degraded one does the opposite, releasing carbon that had been buried for years back into the water and atmosphere. Researchers estimate that degraded seagrass meadows are now releasing somewhere between 154 and 256 gigagrams of carbon dioxide equivalent every year from their biomass alone, with a handful of countries, Australia, Spain, Mexico, Italy and the United States, responsible for more than 80 percent of that total.

Where this connects to the stays on this list

Seagrass sits alongside the same coastal habitats already covered on this list. Mangroves do similar work above the waterline. The two ecosystems often grow within sight of each other along the same protected coastlines.
