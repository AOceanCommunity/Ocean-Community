The Porpoise Down to Fewer Than Ten Animals

Ocean Community·4 min read

The vaquita is the smallest porpoise species in the world and lives in exactly one place: a shallow section of Mexico's upper Gulf of California, an area smaller than many national parks. Nobody has ever hunted a vaquita for its meat, oil or any other product. It is disappearing anyway, caught as unintended bycatch in nets set for a fish most people have never heard of.

A fish bladder worth more than cocaine by weight

The totoaba is a large fish native to the same stretch of the Gulf of California, prized in parts of China for its swim bladder, which is dried and sold as a delicacy and traditional medicine ingredient believed to carry health benefits that have no basis in evidence. A single dried totoaba bladder has sold on the black market for tens of thousands of dollars, a price that has made illegal totoaba fishing extremely profitable despite a decades old ban on catching the species.

A net built for one fish catches another

Totoaba are caught using gillnets, walls of fine mesh that trap a fish by the gills when it tries to swim through. Vaquitas are roughly the same size as a mature totoaba and share the exact same waters, which means a gillnet set for totoaba catches vaquitas just as effectively. A trapped vaquita cannot surface to breathe and drowns within the net, typically within minutes, well before a fishing boat returns to check its lines.

Vaquita numbers fell from an estimated several hundred in the 1990s to fewer than ten individuals by recent surveys, driven almost entirely by gillnet bycatch from an illegal fishery targeting a different species, the totoaba.

A ban that exists on paper more than in practice

Mexico designated the vaquita's entire range a protected refuge and banned gillnet fishing within it, with naval patrols tasked with enforcement. Illegal totoaba fishing has continued largely unabated regardless. The value of a single successful catch can exceed what a fisherman might otherwise earn in months. Patrol coverage across a large area with limited vessels has also proven difficult to make airtight. Conservation groups including Sea Shepherd have run their own vessel patrols and net removal operations in the refuge for years, pulling thousands of illegal gillnets from vaquita habitat.

Why a captive breeding program was abandoned

In 2017, researchers attempted to capture live vaquitas for an emergency captive breeding program, a last resort move given how few individuals remained. The first captured vaquita showed severe stress and was released. The second captured individual died shortly after capture from stress related causes. The program was halted immediately afterward. No vaquita has been held in captivity since, which means the species' survival now depends entirely on protecting the small number of individuals still living wild in the refuge.
