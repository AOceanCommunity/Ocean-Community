The Sargassum Blooms Reshaping Caribbean Coastlines

Ocean Community·4 min read

Sargassum is not new to the Caribbean. What changed over the past decade is the scale of it, large open ocean rafts that used to stay a background feature of the Sargasso Sea now wash onto beaches from Mexico to Belize in volumes that can bury a shoreline knee deep.

A seaweed built to drift, not root

Sargassum is a brown macroalgae that lives its entire life cycle floating on the surface rather than anchored to the seafloor, held up by small gas filled bladders. In small amounts it is a genuinely useful habitat, providing shelter and feeding ground for juvenile fish, sea turtles and a range of species that have evolved around it specifically. The problem is not sargassum existing, it is sargassum arriving at a scale the coastline and the species that depend on normal amounts of it were never built to handle.

A new source feeding the blooms

Since around 2011, a new sargassum belt has formed stretching from West Africa to the Gulf of Mexico, a mass far larger than the traditional Sargasso Sea population. Researchers link the scale of these blooms to nutrient runoff from major river systems, particularly the Amazon and Congo, combined with warming surface water that favors rapid sargassum growth. The result is a seaweed bloom tracked by satellite because it has become large enough to see from space.

What happens once it reaches shore

Sargassum washed onto a beach and left to decompose releases hydrogen sulfide gas, the smell most associated with these bloom seasons and can smother seagrass beds and nesting sites for sea turtles if it accumulates faster than it can be cleared. For coastal communities, the cleanup itself has become a seasonal infrastructure problem, with some destinations running barrier systems offshore specifically to intercept rafts before they reach the beach.

Why this matters for a reef already under pressure

A reef recovering from bleaching or storm damage does not need an additional source of stress layered on top and decomposing sargassum lowers oxygen levels and can smother shallow reef structure the same way sediment runoff does. Turneffe Atoll, protected since 2012 and sitting along this stretch of the Mesoamerican Barrier Reef, is one of the reef systems now dealing with sargassum seasons that did not exist in anything like this scale when the reserve was first declared.
