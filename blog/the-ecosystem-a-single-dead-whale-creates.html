The Ecosystem a Single Dead Whale Creates

Ocean Community·4 min read

Most of a whale's body weight is gone within months of its death, stripped away by sharks, hagfish and other scavengers at the surface or in shallow water. A whale that sinks intact to the deep seafloor instead triggers something researchers did not fully document until submersible technology caught up with the question in the 1980s: a food source so large it can sustain a localized ecosystem for decades.

Three stages, each with its own residents

Marine biologists who study these sites, known as whale falls, describe the process in three overlapping stages. In the first, mobile scavengers including sleeper sharks, hagfish and amphipods strip the soft tissue, a stage that can remove several tons of material within months. In the second, opportunist species colonize the remaining bones and surrounding sediment, feeding on organic material enriched in the seafloor by the carcass. The third and longest stage feeds on the skeleton itself, as bacteria break down lipids locked inside the whale's bones.

Bacteria that run on bone fat instead of sunlight

Whale bones are unusually fatty, storing lipids that most other skeletons do not. Specialized bacteria metabolize those lipids anaerobically, releasing hydrogen sulfide as a byproduct. That chemical becomes the energy source for an entire community of chemosynthetic organisms, including specialized worms, clams and mussels that researchers have found almost nowhere else except whale falls and deep sea hydrothermal vents, two environments that otherwise have little in common.

Researchers have documented whale fall skeletons still supporting a distinct chemosynthetic community more than 50 years after the whale died, among the longest running single food sources known in ocean ecology.

Species that may depend on whale falls to spread at all

Several worm species discovered at whale fall sites, including members of the genus Osedax, have never been found thriving anywhere else, which has led researchers to hypothesize that a scattered network of whale falls across the seafloor may function as stepping stones, letting bone dependent species disperse between isolated sites over evolutionary time. Commercial whaling's dramatic reduction of whale populations through the twentieth century may have cut this network of stepping stones down substantially, a possibility some researchers cite as a reason whale population recovery has value even for species with no obvious connection to whales themselves.

Why finding one at all is mostly luck

Natural whale falls are difficult for researchers to find and study, since a dead whale can sink anywhere across a vast and largely unmapped seafloor. Most of what is known comes from a combination of chance discoveries by submersibles conducting unrelated survey work and a smaller number of deliberately sunk whale carcasses that had already died of natural causes, towed to a chosen research site and monitored over subsequent years with remotely operated cameras.
