Why a Single Whale Is Worth Two Million Dollars in Carbon

Ocean Community·4 min read

A single great whale stores an average of 33 tonnes of carbon dioxide over its lifetime. That is not even the biggest part of its climate value. Whales also fertilize the phytoplankton responsible for capturing a huge share of the planet's carbon.

What a whale actually stores in its body

Great whales sequester an average of 33 tonnes of carbon dioxide each over the course of their lives, carbon locked into their enormous bodies. When a whale dies naturally its body usually sinks to the seafloor in what scientists call a whale fall, trapping that carbon at depth for centuries or even thousands of years rather than releasing it back into the atmosphere.

The whale pump and the conveyor belt

Whales feed at depth and surface to breathe and release waste, a cycle that pulls nutrients like nitrogen, phosphorus and iron up from deep water toward the sunlit surface where phytoplankton can actually use them. Scientists call this the whale pump. Baleen whales add a second mechanism on top of it, migrating as far as 12,000 miles between feeding and breeding grounds and redistributing nutrients across entire ocean basins along the way, sometimes called the great whale conveyor belt.

The International Monetary Fund has valued a single great whale's contribution to carbon capture at more than two million dollars. It values the world's remaining great whale populations at over one trillion dollars combined.

Why phytoplankton is the real payoff

Phytoplankton captures an estimated 37 billion metric tons of carbon dioxide worldwide, something like 40 percent of all the carbon dioxide produced on Earth. Whales do not capture that carbon directly. They fertilize the phytoplankton blooms that do, multiplying their own climate impact well beyond the 33 tonnes stored in any one whale's body.

Where this connects to the stays on this list

The Azores spent nearly two centuries hunting the same sperm whales now recognized for this kind of climate value, before shifting entirely to protecting them instead.
