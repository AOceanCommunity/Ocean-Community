The Abandoned Fishing Gear Still Catching Marine Life

Ocean Community·4 min read

Most fishing gear works exactly as designed, right up until the moment a boat loses track of it. A net or trap does not know the difference. It keeps working, unsupervised, for however long it takes to rot, which for modern synthetic gear can mean years.

What counts as ghost gear

Ghost gear is any fishing equipment, nets, lines, traps and pots, that has been lost, abandoned or discarded at sea rather than retrieved. It happens through storms tearing nets loose, gear snagging on reef or wreckage and sometimes deliberate dumping to avoid the cost or effort of proper disposal. None of those causes change what the gear does next: it keeps fishing, with no one checking the catch.

Why synthetic gear is the real shift

Older fishing gear, made from natural fiber, degraded within a season or two of being lost. Modern gear is overwhelmingly synthetic, nylon and other plastics built specifically to resist exactly the kind of breakdown that would make lost gear stop functioning. That durability is useful on an active fishing boat and becomes the core of the problem the moment that same gear is lost at sea.

Lost and discarded fishing gear is estimated at roughly 640,000 tonnes entering the ocean every year and synthetic nets and lines can keep trapping marine life for years before they finally break down.

Indiscriminate by design

A net set by a crew targets a specific catch and gets hauled in once it has one. A ghost net has no target and no schedule. It drifts or snags in place and keeps entangling whatever swims into it, turtles, sharks, marine mammals and fish well beyond whatever species the gear was originally built to catch, for as long as it continues to function.

What removal work actually looks like

Ghost gear recovery is slow, physical work, usually divers or boat crews locating and hauling out nets snagged on reef structures or drifting in known current patterns. It does not stop gear from being lost in the first place, but every net pulled out is one less indiscriminate trap left running on a reef that often already carries other pressures, from bleaching to tourism traffic.
