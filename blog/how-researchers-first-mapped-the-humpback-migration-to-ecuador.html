How Researchers First Mapped the Humpback Migration to Ecuador

Ocean Community·4 min read

Every year, humpback whales leave the cold, food rich waters off Antarctica and swim thousands of kilometers north to breed in warm, low latitude water. One of the places that route ends is a stretch of Ecuador's Pacific coast near Puerto Lopez and researchers had to actually track individual whales to prove it.

A migration with nothing to eat at the other end

Humpback whales feed almost exclusively in cold, nutrient rich polar waters, building up fat reserves they live off for months. The warm water they migrate to for breeding and calving is largely barren of the krill and small fish they depend on. The whole migration is a tradeoff: better conditions for a newborn calf, at the cost of months without feeding for the adults making the trip.

Proving it with photographs, not assumptions

Every humpback whale has a unique pattern on the underside of its tail fluke, as individual as a fingerprint. Researchers build photo identification catalogs of these fluke patterns and match sightings against them over years, which is how a single whale photographed off Antarctica can later be confirmed as the same animal photographed breeding off Ecuador months later. That matching is what turns a plausible migration route into a documented one.

Humpback whales migrating along this route travel thousands of kilometers between Antarctic feeding grounds and breeding waters off Ecuador and neighboring Pacific coastlines, one of the longest mammal migrations on Earth.

Ongoing work, not a closed case

Mapping a migration route once does not mean the work stops. Researchers keep tracking the same population to watch for changes, in timing, in numbers, in the routes themselves, since all three can shift as ocean conditions change. The Pacific Whale Foundation has continued running humpback whale research and photo identification work in these waters since the migration route was first mapped.
