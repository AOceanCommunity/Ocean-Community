The Rules That Govern Whale Shark Tourism in the Sea of Cortez

Ocean Community·4 min read

Bahía de los Ángeles sits on a stretch of the Gulf of California remote enough that it still feels like a secret, except to the whale sharks that gather there every year.

Why the sharks show up here at all

The Gulf of California, also known as the Sea of Cortez, is unusually productive water for its latitude. Strong tidal mixing and seasonal upwelling push nutrients toward the surface, feeding the plankton blooms that whale sharks filter feed on. Bahía de los Ángeles is one of several bays along the gulf where juvenile whale sharks in particular gather in predictable numbers, making it one of the more reliable places in the world to see them close to shore.

A juvenile aggregation, not a random sighting

Most of the whale sharks seen in bays like this one are juveniles, not fully grown adults. Researchers who track individual sharks by their unique spot patterns have found some return to the same bay across multiple seasons, which is part of why these aggregation sites get treated as distinct, trackable populations rather than just a spot where sharks happen to pass through.

Encounters with whale sharks and sea lions in Bahía de los Ángeles run under government wildlife regulation, covering how boats and swimmers are allowed to approach the animals.

What regulation actually covers

Whale shark tourism in Mexican waters operates under federal wildlife rules that set limits on how many boats and swimmers can approach a single shark at once, minimum distances and a ban on touching the animals. The rules exist because whale sharks filter feeding at the surface are easy to crowd and a bay that gets too much uncontrolled boat traffic can push the animals to stop using it.

Las Animas Ecolodge's place on this list

Las Animas Ecolodge is a remote, boat access only lodge built around these regulated whale shark, whale and sea lion encounters.
