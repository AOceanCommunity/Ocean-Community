Why Shipping Lanes Keep Killing the Rarest Whale

Ocean Community·4 min read

The North Atlantic right whale is named for the reason whalers once hunted it almost to nothing: it swims slowly near the surface, floats when killed and was, in that sense, the "right" whale to pursue. The species survived that era by a margin thin enough that its population has never recovered. Fewer than 370 remain. The leading confirmed cause of death today is not a harpoon but a ship's hull.

A whale built for a coastline it no longer controls

Right whales feed by swimming slowly through dense patches of tiny crustaceans called copepods, often at or near the surface, along a migratory route that runs through some of the busiest shipping corridors on the US east coast. Their feeding and calving grounds overlap almost exactly with the approaches to major ports including Boston, New York and Savannah. A whale moving at a few miles an hour has little ability to avoid a container ship moving at 15 or 20 knots. The whale often has no warning that the ship is coming at all.

Two causes account for most confirmed deaths

Since 2017, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration has tracked an "Unusual Mortality Event" for the species, a formal designation triggered once deaths and serious injuries exceed a statistical threshold. Vessel strikes and entanglement in fishing gear together account for the large majority of confirmed deaths and injuries recorded under that designation. Both causes are, in principle, preventable through speed limits and gear modifications, which is what makes the continuing toll especially difficult for regulators to explain.

Fewer than 370 North Atlantic right whales remain. Vessel strikes and fishing gear entanglement together account for most of the confirmed deaths tracked since NOAA declared an Unusual Mortality Event for the species in 2017.

The speed rule and why it is hard to enforce

NOAA has required vessels 65 feet or longer to slow to 10 knots within designated seasonal management areas along the migratory route since 2008, a rule credited with reducing lethal strike risk where it is actually followed. Compliance has been inconsistent. Vessel tracking studies published by conservation groups have repeatedly found that a meaningful share of large ships transit these zones above the mandated speed, since the rule currently applies to a defined vessel size class and specific zones rather than covering all vessel traffic along the full range of the whale's habitat.

A population that cannot absorb many more losses

Right whale calving rates have declined over the past decade even as researchers work to reduce direct mortality, a combination that keeps the population on a downward trajectory despite decades of protection under the Endangered Species Act. Researchers estimate the species can sustain the loss of fewer than one individual per year from human causes before the population begins shrinking. Current average losses run well above that number most years.
