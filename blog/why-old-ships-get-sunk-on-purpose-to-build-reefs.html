Why Old Ships Get Sunk on Purpose to Build Reefs

Ocean Community·4 min read

An aircraft carrier, a decommissioned warship or a retired ferry is an enormous object to dispose of. Scrapping one properly is expensive. A landfill has no use for a 900 foot hull. Sinking it in a planned location, as an artificial reef, is sometimes the cheaper option even before counting what it gives back to the ocean.

The cleanup costs more than the ship

A vessel cannot simply be towed out and sunk. Regulations in most countries require stripping out fuel, oil, wiring insulation, foam, loose debris and anything containing PCBs or other contaminants before the hull touches the water, a process that can take months and cost more than the ship itself was worth as scrap metal. The 2006 sinking of the USS Oriskany off the coast of Florida, at the time the largest vessel ever sunk as an artificial reef, required an extensive multi-year cleanup overseen by the Environmental Protection Agency before the Navy was permitted to proceed.

Why bare metal attracts a reef at all

A clean steel hull sitting on open sand provides something a flat seafloor does not: vertical structure. Within weeks, algae and bacteria form a biofilm across exposed surfaces, followed by barnacles, sponges and soft coral larvae settling onto those same surfaces over the following months. Fish arrive almost immediately for shelter, long before any permanent coral growth has established, since the structure itself provides refuge from predators regardless of what has colonized it yet.

Cleaning a ship to safety standards for sinking can take longer and cost more than scrapping it for metal. The 2006 sinking of the USS Oriskany required a multi-year, EPA-supervised cleanup before it was approved.

Not a direct swap for a natural reef

Marine biologists are careful to note that an artificial reef does not replace a natural one. A wreck develops a different community than a natural reef nearby, often dominated by species suited to hard vertical surfaces rather than the full diversity found on a mature coral structure built up over centuries. Artificial reefs work best as an addition to existing protected habitat or as a way to relieve diving pressure on a fragile natural reef by giving divers an alternative destination, not as mitigation that offsets damage done elsewhere.

Placement decides whether it helps or just sits there

Where a wreck goes matters as much as what it is made of. Placed on bare sand in a current that carries larvae past regularly, a wreck can develop meaningful reef structure within a decade. Placed somewhere with poor water circulation or little larval supply nearby, the same ship can sit for years as mostly bare metal with limited colonization. Some projects, including several of Florida's purpose sunk vessels, were selected specifically for their position relative to existing reef systems and known current patterns, rather than simply picked for convenience of towing distance from port.
