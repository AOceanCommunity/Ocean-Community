Inside the World's Largest Shark Sanctuary

Ocean Community·5 min read

Oceanic shark and ray populations have fallen by 71 percent since 1970. French Polynesia took a different path than most, banning shark fishing across the whole of its ocean territory rather than managing catch limits species by species.

How steep the decline actually is

A study tracking oceanic sharks and rays over five decades found a 71 percent drop in global abundance since 1970, driven by an eighteen fold increase in fishing pressure over the same period. More than 100 shark species lost over 80 percent of their population between 1975 and 2009. The most recent global assessment puts roughly a third of all sharks, rays and related species at risk of extinction.

Why losing sharks changes everything below them

Sharks sit at the top of the ocean food web. Removing them does not just mean fewer sharks. Their absence lets prey populations grow unchecked, which then puts pressure on the smaller species and habitats those prey animals rely on. Scientists call this a trophic cascade, a chain reaction that starts with removing a predator and ends up reshaping an entire ecosystem.

French Polynesia's shark sanctuary covers roughly 5.5 million square kilometers of ocean, making it the largest protected area for sharks anywhere in the world.

One country's different approach

In April 2006 French Polynesia declared its entire ocean territory a sanctuary for sharks and rays, banning commercial fishing of nearly every species across roughly 5.5 million square kilometers of ocean, an area about half the size of Canada. One species, the shortfin mako, was left out of the original law and only added in 2012, completing protection for every shark and ray species in French Polynesian waters.
