What Coral Bleaching Actually Does to a Reef

Ocean Community·5 min read

Coral bleaching sounds like a simple color change. In a sense it is, but the mechanism behind it is closer to starvation. Four global bleaching events have hit progressively larger shares of the world's reefs since 1998.

What actually happens inside the coral

Coral gets most of its color and much of its food from algae living inside its tissue. When water temperatures rise by as little as one degree Celsius above the normal seasonal average for several weeks, the coral expels that algae as a stress response. Without it the coral turns white, loses its main food source and can starve if warm conditions do not ease in time.

Four events, each bigger than the last

The first global bleaching event in 1998 affected an estimated 21 percent of the world's reefs. The second in 2010 reached 37 percent. The third, running from 2014 to 2017, hit 68 percent. The fourth event, running from 2023 into 2025, reached roughly 84 percent of the world's reefs across 82 countries and territories, the largest and most widespread bleaching event ever recorded.

The most recent global bleaching event, running from 2023 into 2025, affected roughly 84 percent of the world's reefs, more than four times the share hit by the first recorded event in 1998.

Recovery is possible but not guaranteed

Coral can regain its algae and recover if temperatures drop back to normal quickly enough. One study of an especially variable reef environment found coral in more exposed, temperature swinging areas recovered within six months, while coral in steadier, less variable conditions nearby died at a rate of 68 percent during the same bleaching event. Repeated bleaching events also leave surviving coral more vulnerable to disease and less able to reproduce afterward.

Why this matters for the stays on this list

Several properties on this list sit beside reefs that have already been through this. Six Senses Laamu's own marine research team responded to the most recent bleaching event by growing coral back onto damaged reef, larva by larva. Raja Ampat holds some of the highest coral diversity on Earth, which gives it more resilience than most reefs but does not make it immune.
