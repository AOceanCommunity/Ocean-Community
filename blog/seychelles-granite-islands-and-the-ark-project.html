Seychelles, an Island Rebuilt for the Ocean Around It

Ocean Community·5 min read

North Island Seychelles is barefoot luxury now, but for most of the last century it was a coconut plantation, cleared of its native forest and farmed hard. The reefs around it inherited the runoff. Its Ark Project is a slow attempt to put both back.

Islands unlike anywhere else

The granitic Seychelles are the only mid oceanic granite islands on Earth, fragments of an ancient landmass that broke away from other continents more than 75 million years ago and drifted alone in the Indian Ocean ever since. The huge smoothed boulders that show up on nearly every postcard of the islands are the visible part of that geology. What is less visible is what centuries of plantation farming did to the coastline and the water just offshore.

A plantation, then a restoration

North Island was farmed for coconuts for generations before it was set aside for conservation and turned into a private reserve with a small number of villas attached. The Ark Project is the name for the restoration effort that followed, replacing plantation land with native rainforest and reintroducing species that had disappeared from the island, most visibly giant tortoises.

North Island was among the first private islands anywhere to seek international marine protection status for the reefs surrounding it. Green and hawksbill turtles, absent for years, are now returning to nest on its beaches.

Why land restoration shows up in the water

Coconut plantations strip the ground cover that normally holds soil in place and heavy rain on cleared land sends sediment straight into the nearshore reef, smothering coral that needs clear water to survive. Rebuilding native forest on North Island was never only about the tortoises. Stable ground cover means less runoff, which is part of why turtles have started nesting again on beaches that had gone quiet for years.
