Why Medicine Still Depends on Horseshoe Crab Blood

Ocean Community·4 min read

Horseshoe crabs have survived almost unchanged for roughly 450 million years, long enough to outlast several mass extinctions. One property of their blood, copper based and pale blue instead of iron based and red, has made them essential to a part of modern medicine that has nothing to do with their own biology: testing whether an injectable drug is contaminated with bacteria.

A blood cell that clots around bacteria

Horseshoe crab blood contains cells called amebocytes that clot almost instantly on contact with bacterial toxins, an immune response that evolved as the animal's primary defense against infection in a world without the antibodies most other animals rely on. In the 1960s, researchers isolated a clotting agent from these cells, called Limulus Amebocyte Lysate or LAL. It reacted to even trace amounts of bacterial contamination with a visible gel clot. The pharmaceutical industry adopted LAL testing in the late 1970s as the standard method for confirming that vaccines, injectable drugs and implantable medical devices are free of bacterial contamination before they reach a patient.

What a bleeding facility actually does

Commercial bleeding facilities, concentrated along the US Atlantic coast, collect wild horseshoe crabs during their spring spawning season, extract roughly 30 percent of each crab's blood volume through a needle near the heart, then return the animals to the water. Industry estimates put annual mortality from the bleeding process itself at around 15 percent, though some independent research suggests the true figure, including crabs that die later from the stress of capture and handling, may run higher.

Around half a million horseshoe crabs are bled annually for LAL testing, the global standard method for confirming injectable drugs are free of bacterial contamination. Industry estimated mortality from the process is around 15 percent.

A synthetic alternative that exists but isn't required

A synthetic substitute called recombinant Factor C, developed in the 1990s and manufactured without any animal blood, has been validated as equally effective in independent studies and is already approved for use in Europe. Most major US and global pharmacopoeias have been slower to formally accept it as an equivalent standard, which means many manufacturers continue defaulting to LAL testing even where a synthetic option is legally available, largely due to the cost and regulatory complexity of switching an established testing protocol.

The bird that depends on the same crabs

Horseshoe crab eggs are also the primary food source for red knots, a shorebird that migrates from the southern tip of South America to the Arctic each year and times its stopover at Delaware Bay to coincide with the crab spawning season. Red knot populations have declined sharply over the past several decades, a trend conservationists link in part to reduced horseshoe crab egg availability from a combination of bleeding mortality, bait harvesting for the eel and whelk fishing industries and coastal habitat loss along the same beaches both species depend on.
