What Reef Safe Sunscreen Actually Means

Ocean Community·4 min read

Two common sunscreen ingredients are toxic enough to coral that entire destinations have banned them outright. Here is what actually happens to a reef when they wash off in the water.

The two ingredients behind the bans

Oxybenzone and octinoxate are UV filters used in a large share of conventional sunscreens, present in roughly 32 percent of sunscreens sold before Hawaii's ban took effect. Only about 4 percent of the oxybenzone applied to skin actually stays there. The remaining 96 percent washes off into the water, mostly in exactly the tourist bays and reef areas where people swim.

What it actually does to coral

Research has linked oxybenzone and octinoxate exposure to coral bleaching, DNA damage in coral tissue and disrupted reproduction in fish. The chemicals also affect free swimming coral larvae directly, making it harder for a damaged reef to repopulate itself even after other stressors ease. An estimated 14,000 tons of sunscreen wash into the ocean worldwide every year.

Roughly 96 percent of the oxybenzone applied to skin ends up in the water rather than staying on the body, much of it in the same reef areas swimmers visit.

Where it has actually been banned

Hawaii passed a ban on selling sunscreens containing oxybenzone and octinoxate in 2018, with the law taking effect in 2021. Palau went further that same year, banning ten chemicals found harmful to its reefs and marine life, with fines in place for vendors who keep selling banned products.

What actually counts as reef safe

A sunscreen labeled reef safe is not a regulated term anywhere, so the label alone guarantees very little. Checking the ingredient list for oxybenzone and octinoxate specifically, rather than trusting a marketing label, is the only way to know what is actually going into the water at any given reef.
