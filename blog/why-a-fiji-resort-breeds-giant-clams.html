Why a Fiji Resort Breeds Giant Clams

Ocean Community·4 min read

A giant clam does not look like a species anyone needed to rescue. They have survived largely by sitting still. That stillness is exactly why Pacific populations collapsed so fast once people started diving for them, and why one Fiji resort now breeds them by hand.

A reef animal that farms its own food

Giant clams, the genus Tridacna, host photosynthetic algae in their mantle tissue, the same kind of algae that live inside coral. The clam gets a share of the sugars the algae produce from sunlight, which is why giant clams need shallow, clear water to survive and why their mantles show such vivid color. A single clam can live for decades, and some species grow large enough to weigh over 200 kilograms.

Easy to find, which was the problem

A giant clam cannot swim away. That made them simple to harvest for meat and for their shells, and Pacific populations were fished down hard through the twentieth century, severely enough that several Tridacna species are now listed as vulnerable on the IUCN Red List. Entire reef systems lost their clam populations almost completely in places.

Giant clams cannot relocate if a reef is overharvested. Across parts of the Pacific, that made them one of the easiest reef species to fish to local extinction, and one of the hardest to bring back without deliberate breeding programs.

Breeding them back, one cohort at a time

Captive breeding programs for giant clams work by spawning adults in controlled conditions, raising the larvae past their most vulnerable stage, and growing juveniles in protected nursery sites before moving them back onto the reef. It is slow work. Clams grow a few centimeters a year at best, and a breeding program only shows results once those juveniles survive long enough to reproduce themselves.

Jean Michel Cousteau Resort's place on this list

Jean Michel Cousteau Resort, founded on Jacques Cousteau's son's own ocean conservation philosophy, keeps a full time marine biologist on staff and runs a giant clam breeding project in Savusavu Bay alongside its reef protection program.
