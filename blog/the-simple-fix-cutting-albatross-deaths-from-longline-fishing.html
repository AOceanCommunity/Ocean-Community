The Simple Fix Cutting Albatross Deaths From Longline Fishing

Ocean Community·4 min read

A longline fishing vessel can set tens of thousands of baited hooks on a single line stretching up to 60 miles behind the boat. Albatrosses and other seabirds dive for that bait as it sinks, swallow a hook and drown. The practice has been a leading driver of decline for albatross species worldwide. The fix that cuts the death toll most effectively costs a fishing operation almost nothing to adopt.

A bird built for distance, not for avoiding hooks

Albatrosses can travel thousands of miles on a single foraging trip, soaring on wind currents with barely a wingbeat. That same adaptation makes them efficient at covering open ocean in search of an easy meal. A freshly baited longline sinking behind a boat looks exactly like one. Researchers studying tracking data from tagged birds have found that fishing vessels can draw albatrosses in from many miles away, concentrating birds in the exact zone where hooks are most dangerous.

Three cheap changes that work together

Fisheries scientists have identified a small set of mitigation measures that, used together, dramatically reduce seabird bycatch without reducing the catch of the target fish. Bird scaring lines, long streamers towed above the baited hooks, startle birds away from the water where hooks are sinking. Setting lines at night, when most albatross species forage less actively, removes much of the overlap entirely. Weighting the line so hooks sink faster shrinks the window during which bait is available near the surface.

Longline fishing is estimated to kill around 100,000 seabirds a year worldwide. Vessels using bird scaring lines, night setting and weighted lines together have recorded bycatch reductions of more than 90 percent.

Why adoption still lags behind the evidence

The mitigation measures are cheap, well studied and required by law in several national fishing fleets and in some regional fisheries management agreements. Enforcement at sea is difficult, since observer coverage on longline vessels remains low across much of the global fleet. A boat operating far from port in international waters faces a real chance that noncompliance simply goes unnoticed. Illegal, unreported and unregulated fishing operations, which by definition ignore most regulations, account for a disproportionate share of the remaining seabird bycatch.

A recovery that depends on consistent practice

The Black-browed Albatross and several related species remain classified as threatened by the International Union for Conservation of Nature, a status driven heavily by longline bycatch over past decades. Albatrosses are also slow breeders, typically raising a single chick every one to two years and not reaching breeding age themselves for several years, which means a population that loses too many adults to bycatch can take decades to recover even after fishing practices improve.
